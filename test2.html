Lesson 4: Graphs

Glossary Terms

Graph

A visual way of showing data so patterns, comparisons and relationships are easier to see.

X-axis

The horizontal axis. In a fair test line graph, this often shows the independent variable — what was changed.

Y-axis

The vertical axis. In a fair test graph, this often shows the dependent variable — what was measured.

Line graph

A graph that uses points and lines to show how a measured value changes across ordered or continuous values.

Bar graph

A graph that uses separate bars to compare amounts across different categories.

Pie chart

A circle divided into slices. Each slice shows how much one category contributes to the whole.

Turning Data into Graphs

Graphs help scientists turn a table of numbers into a picture that makes patterns, comparisons and parts of a whole easier to see.

Prototype: all three graph activities are visible at once so we can compare ideas. The examples use simple science-style data and can be swapped later.

Learning objective

We are learning how to choose, build and read different kinds of graphs.

Success criteria

  1. I can explain what the x-axis and y-axis show.
  2. I can use a line graph or bar graph to represent data.
  3. I can explain how a pie chart shows parts of one whole.

Think like a scientist

Different graphs answer different questions

A graph is not just decoration. Scientists choose a graph based on what they want the reader to notice.

📈 Change

Use a line graph when the order between x-values matters and you want to see a trend.

📊 Compare

Use a bar graph when you want to compare separate categories.

🥧 Whole

Use a pie chart when all categories are parts of the same total.

Yeast activity

Hover or click a plotted point to read its coordinates.

Line graph

A point means two things at once

The dotted guides let students trace from a point back to its x-value and y-value.

The line comes last

Students see the raw points before the line, so the line is understood as a way of following change.

Good for ordered values

Temperature has a meaningful order, so moving from 10 → 20 → 30 °C makes sense.

2. Compare Categories with a Bar Graph

A bar graph answers a different question: which category has more, less, highest or lowest? The bars stay separate because the categories are separate.

Build the bars from the data

Example: average bounce height of the same ball on four different surfaces.

22Carpet · cm
34Grass · cm
63Wood · cm
78Tile · cm
Why start at zero?
Bar height is what we compare, so using zero as the baseline keeps the visual comparison fair.
Look at the categories first. Carpet, grass, wood and tile are separate groups, so each one gets its own bar.

Ball bounce height

Build the bars one at a time, then compare their heights.

Bar graph

Bars are separate

The gap signals that carpet and grass are different categories, not values between each other.

Height carries meaning

Taller bar = larger amount. Students can compare before reading the exact numbers.

Ask comparison questions

Which is highest? Lowest? How much higher? These are natural bar-graph questions.

3. Turn a Whole into a Pie Chart

A pie chart is about part of a whole. Here the whole is a class of 20 students. Add each group as a slice and watch the chart fill toward 100%.

20 students = one whole class

Example class poll: Which way of investigating science do students enjoy most?

8Observe
6Measure
4Experiment
2Research
How much of the whole have we added?

0 of 20 students = 0%

Start with the whole. Every student belongs to exactly one category, so all four groups together must make 20 students, or 100%.

Favourite investigation style

After slices are added, click a legend item to inspect one part.

Pie chart
Select a slice. Once the pie is built, choose a category to see its fraction of the whole and its percentage.

The total must be meaningful

A pie chart only makes sense when all slices belong to the same whole.

Slice size means proportion

8 of 20 students becomes 40% of the circle; 2 of 20 becomes 10%.

Everything adds to 100%

The fill meter makes the idea of completing the whole explicit before the finished pie appears.
