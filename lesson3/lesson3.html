Lesson 3: Tables

Glossary Terms

Data

The observations and measurements you collect during an investigation. Data can be numbers (e.g. temperature, height) or descriptions (e.g. colour, texture).

Qualitative data

Data that describes what something looks, feels, sounds, smells or tastes like. It uses words rather than numbers (e.g. the bread mould is green and fuzzy).

Quantitative data

Data that uses numbers to measure something (e.g. the ball bounced 45cm high).

Repeat trial

Carrying out the same test more than once under exactly the same conditions to check that your results are consistent.

Lesson 3

Tables

Scientists organise results in data tables so that measurements are clear, consistent and easy to compare.

Learning objective

We are learning how to collect and organise data into a table.

Success criteria

  1. I can label a table with the variables and units in the correct format.
  2. I can collect data and enter it into a table with the correct units.
  3. I can summarise the results of an experiment using averages.

Meet the yeast balloon experiment

Before we look at the results

How does temperature affect yeast activity?

Yeast is a tiny living organism that you can only see under a microscope. Even though it is tiny, yeast is alive and needs food to survive.

When yeast eats sugar, it breaks it down through a process called fermentation. This releases carbon dioxide gas.

What did we do?

We mixed yeast, sugar and water in a flask, then stretched a balloon over the top to trap the gas that was produced.

If the yeast was very active, it produced more gas and the balloon inflated more. We measured the circumference of the balloon to compare the results.

Yeast experiment: calculate the average

Repeating a test gives us several results. An average combines those results into one value that is easier to compare.

To calculate the average, add the three test results together and divide the total by 3.
