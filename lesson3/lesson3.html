Lesson 3: Tables

Glossary Terms

Data

The observations and measurements you collect during an investigation. Data can be numbers (e.g. temperature, height) or descriptions (e.g. colour, texture).

Qualitative data

Data that describes what something looks, feels, sounds, smells or tastes like. It uses words rather than numbers (e.g. the bread mould is green and fuzzy).

Quantitative data

Data that uses numbers to measure something (e.g. the ball bounced 45cm high).

Repeat trial

Carrying out the same test more than once under exactly the same conditions to check that your results are consistent.

The Story of Yeast Y

Hover over each image to learn more about the activity of yeast and why it is important to produce sourdough bread.
1

Dry yeast is sold in packets. Yeast is a tiny living organism that you can only see under a microscope! Even though it is tiny, yeast is alive, and just like other living things, it needs food to survive. The yeast in these packets is dormant. To wake it up you need to give it water, sugar and the right temperature to make it active.

2

Sugar is used by yeast as food to get energy so it can grow and reproduce.

3

When you mix dry yeast and sugar in water it will start to become active. Active yeast produces and releases carbon dioxide gas. This gas can be seen as bubbles in the mixture. The gas bubbles rise to the surface and pop, so the gas escapes the mixture and enters the air around it.

4

Active yeast produces and releases carbon dioxide gas. This makes bread dough rise. As bread is baked the bubbles of gas get trapped, making holes in the bread so it is light and soft to eat.

Why is it important to repeat each test 3 times?

In a fair test experiment, we repeat each test 3 times using the same conditions.

We do this to:

  • Check for mistakes when setting up each test.
  • Check for errors when making measurements.
  • Spot any strange results.
  • Make the results more reliable and trustworthy.

Example

Three tennis-ball bounce tests

Look at the results from three repeats of the same tennis-ball test.

Look at the diagram above. A fair test was repeated 3 times to see how high a tennis ball bounces on grass.

Why is it important to repeat a test 3 times?

What would happen if you only did the first test?

Repeating a test helps us check whether the results are consistent and makes it easier to spot mistakes or unusual results. If we only used one result, that result might be unusual or affected by an error, so our conclusion could be misleading.
